# Memo: Budgets explain 5.1% of why some colleges beat the odds on first-generation completion. Look at the peers.

**To:** Director of Student Success · **From:** Isiah Luna · **Date:** 2026-10-26

## How much of over-performance is money?

Not much. Among 1,198 four-year colleges, the top tenth graduated first-gen students at least 11.2 points above what their student mix predicts. Spending level and mix together explain **5.1%** of the variation in that residual. With the budget size held fixed, one standard deviation more academic-support share is associated with +0.9 points and one SD more instruction share with +0.7; doubling core spending per student is associated with about +3 points. All three are real (p = 0.004, 0.047, <0.001) and small against an 11.2-point gap. Most over-performance sits outside budget data, so the recommendation is a list of schools to call, not a reallocation.

## What the budget data do show

Over-performers spent more per student; the pattern differs by sector. Publics, ranked against publics: more on everything (instruction 1.25×, academic support 1.48×, student services 1.28×). Privates: instruction 1.44× and academic support 1.36×, student services flat (1.03×). After correcting for 100 comparisons, the instruction and academic-support gaps survive everywhere; the student-services gap nowhere.

**Method.** Completion of first-gen students at the same school within 6 years, predicted from 2017-18 Pell share, admission rate, enrollment, part-time, first-gen, adult and online shares, HBCU and Carnegie type, with slopes that differ by sector (R² 0.55). Residual = actual − predicted, shrunk for small cohorts.

## Limitations

1. **School-level data.** We see institutions, not students; program mix or unmeasured preparation can produce a residual unrelated to practice. Special-focus, adult and online campuses are listed separately.
2. **Correlation only.** Nothing here shows that moving dollars would change completion.
3. **Definition.** Transfer-out completers count as non-completers; privacy suppression removes the smallest schools.
